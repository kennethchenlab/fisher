RNA regulation in childhood cancer

Genes are expressed as messenger RNA in a highly dynamic process. For this reason, living cells have a variety of tools for regulating RNA synthesis and degradation. Occasionally, loss of certain components of this regulation can lead to diseases such as childhood cancers. For example, loss of one of these tools, known as "microRNAs", can lead to a variety of rare childhood tumors, such as pleuropulmonary blastoma, Wilms tumor, and rhabdomyosarcoma. Mutations in another regulatory tool, the chromatin remodeling complexes, can lead to leukemias, bone and muscle cancers, and kidney cancers.

We study how dysregulation of RNA synthesis and degradation drives childhood cancers, with the goal of identifying new therapeutic vulnerabilities to exploit in treating them.

Read on to learn more.

Tumor formation

Wilms tumor is the most common pediatric kidney cancer. Many are driven by mutations that impair gene regulation, both before mRNA production (transcription factors and chromatin remodeling factors) and after (such as in microRNA processing genes). We are studying how the mutations that arise in Wilms tumors affect gene expression and drive tumor formation. Additionally, we are continuing to identify more mechanisms of gene dysregulation in Wilms tumor.

 

 

 

DICER1 mutations

One particular gene which regulates gene expression, known as DICER1, can be mutated in wide range of rare cancers. In fact, some children are born with mutations in DICER1 that predispose them to developing cancer. However, no therapies have been developed for DICER1-related cancers. We seek to understand how DICER1 cancers arise, using both in vitro and in vivo models, in order to design new therapies for them.

 

 

 

Chemoresistance

Dactinomycin, an inhibitor of transcription (RNA production), is one of the most commonly used chemotherapy drugs for childhood cancers including Wilms tumor, rhabdomyosarcoma, and pleuropulmonary blastoma. It is effective much of the time, but there are usually no effective options for tumors resistant to front-line therapy. We are investigating how tumors evade transcriptional inhibition and ways to target these escape mechanisms.
